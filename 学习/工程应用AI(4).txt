A B S T R A C T
To address the computational bottleneck of deep learning, a key artificial intelligence (AI) technology, in 
resource-constrained Battery Management Systems (BMS), this paper presents a lightweight, linear-complexity 
State of Health (SOH) estimation framework. First, a systematic feature selection process identifies robust 
health indicators from partial voltage–time integrals, effectively reducing online extraction burdens. Subse-
quently, we propose SL-AgentNet (integrating Small-kernel and Large-kernel convolutions), an AI architecture 
designed to resolve the quadratic overhead of traditional Transformers. By integrating the Fast Local–
Global Fusion Attention (FLFA) mechanism with diversity-enhancing modules, specifically the Fast Diversity 
Feature Fusion Attention (FDFA) and Parallel Informed Activation Function (PIAF), this AI model achieves 
linear complexity while capturing long-range degradation dependencies. Experiments on the Oxford, National 
Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), and Center for Advanced Life Cycle Engineering (CALCE) 
datasets demonstrate superior robustness, reducing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by 74.78% on the Oxford 
dataset against Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-Transformer baselines. With a model size of only 
40.92 Kilobytes (KB), the AI framework proves its feasibility through hardware-in-the-loop validation on a 
Texas Instruments (TI) Cortex-M4 microcontroller, where a Look-Up Table (LUT) implementation maintains 
accuracy loss within 0.3%, demonstrating its readiness for embedded deployment.

tion for zinc anodes (Hu et al., 2024) and movable SiO2 reinforced 
coatings (Wu et al., 2025). Simultaneously, at the grid system level, 
new source-load coordinated optimization frameworks have been pro-
posed to enhance distributed energy efficiency (Wang et al., 2025). 
Concurrently with these emerging technologies, Lithium-ion batteries 
(LIBs) currently remain the primary power source for Electric Vehicles 
(EVs). However, as LIB technology evolves towards higher energy 
densities (e.g., high-nickel cathodes, cell-to-pack (CTP) architectures),

The proliferation of deep learning has facilitated the widespread 
adoption of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neu-
ral Networks (RNNs) to capture complex aging dynamics. Nevertheless, 
CNNs are limited by their local receptive fields, while RNNs suf-

While the Transformer architecture (Vaswani et al., 2017) excels at cap-

self-attention entails quadratic computational complexity 𝑂(𝑁2) with

This substantial computational overhead is often challenging for 
automotive-grade microcontrollers (MCUs) (Xiong et al., 2019). Fur-
thermore, deep Transformer models may be prone to the rank collapse 
issue (Chen, 2023), a phenomenon where output representations be-
come identical across tokens. This convergence may lead to the loss of 
fine-grained local degradation details critical for early fault detection.
To address these challenges, this paper proposes SL-AgentNet, a 
lightweight and efficient SOH estimation framework. In contrast to 
many existing methods that pursue structural complexity, the proposed 
approach aims to balance engineering feasibility with accuracy. The 
main contributions are:
1. Structured Feature Selection Framework: We propose a data-driven 
screening methodology based on the Constant Current Charg-
ing/Discharging Area (CCCA/CCDA). In contrast to static fea-
ture selection, this framework treats the observation window 
as a learnable hyperparameter, adaptively identifying a dataset-
specific optimized interval. This facilitates the extraction of a 
robust Health Indicator (HI) that reduces the online computation 
burden while enhancing correlation with capacity fade.
2. Integrated Lightweight Architecture: We introduce the SL-AgentNet, 
featuring a tri-branch design. The core Fast Local–Global Fusion 
Attention (FLFA) reduces complexity from quadratic 𝑂(𝑁2) to 
linear 𝑂(𝑁) by employing a single-agent mechanism (𝑛= 1) 
with convolutional compensation. To mitigate potential feature 
collapse, we integrate the Fast Diversity Feature Fusion Atten-
tion (FDFA) for orthogonal significance filtering and the Parallel 
Informed Activation Function (PIAF) for heterogeneous non-
linear mapping. This architecture aims to achieve enhanced 
representational capability with a compact parameter set.
3. Validation of Feasibility and Reliability: Comprehensive experi-
ments on the Oxford, NASA, and CALCE datasets demonstrate 
that SL-AgentNet achieves competitive accuracy, exemplified by 
an RMSE of 0.0052 on NASA, even when trained on 30% of 
early-life data. Furthermore, we provide a rigorous analysis of 
computational efficiency and uncertainty quantification. Vali-
dated on a TI Cortex-M4 MCU, the proposed LUT-based imple-
mentation achieves negligible precision loss (< 0.3%), proving 
its feasibility for resource-constrained embedded instrumenta-
tion.
2. Related work
Methodologies for battery SOH estimation can be classified into 
model-based and data-driven approaches Xie et al. (2024). This sec-
tion reviews representative techniques in each category and analyzes 
their specific limitations within the context of vehicular embedded 
applications.
2.1. Model-based approaches
Model-based methods are based on mathematical representations of 
the battery’s internal electrochemical or electrical dynamics.
• Electrochemical Models (EMs): Models such as the Single Particle 
Model (SPM) and Pseudo-Two-Dimensional (P2D) model describe 
internal degradation mechanisms, including solid electrolyte in-
terphase (SEI) growth and lithium plating, utilizing systems of 
Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) (Birkl et al., 2017). While 
offering high fidelity and physical interpretability, their high com-
putational complexity and the difficulty of identifying numerous 
microscopic parameters online may render them less suitable 
for low-cost, resource-constrained BMS MCUs (Chaturvedi et al., 
2010; Rechkemmer et al., 2019).
• Equivalent Circuit Models (ECMs): ECMs simulate battery dynamics 
using electrical components, such as resistors and capacitors, and 
are frequently combined with adaptive filters like Kalman Filters 
(KF) or Particle Filters (PF) (Zhang et al., 2018; Peng et al., 
2025). Although computationally lighter than EMs, their accuracy 
depends on the fidelity of the circuit structure. Furthermore, ECM 
parameters exhibit high sensitivity to environmental conditions, 
including temperature and C-rate (charge/discharge rate), and 
maintaining robustness across the full aging lifespan remains a 
significant challenge (Luo et al., 2022).
2.2. Data-driven approaches
Data-driven methods treat the battery as a black box, mapping 
extracted features directly to SOH. These methods have gained promi-
nence due to their flexibility Richardson et al. (2017) and decoupling 
from complex physical parameters. Recent work has also emphasized 
security considerations in battery state estimation Tian et al. (2023).
2.2.1. Feature engineering: Health indicators (HIs)
The efficacy of data-driven models is closely linked to the quality 
of input features.
• Differential Analysis: Incremental Capacity Analysis (ICA) and Dif-
ferential Voltage Analysis (DVA) are widely employed to identify 
degradation modes (z. Kong et al., 2021). Alternative approaches 
exploit differential temperature Tian et al. (2020) or entropy-
based metrics Widodo et al. (2011) to characterize degrada-
tion. However, mathematically, calculating derivatives (𝑑𝑄∕𝑑𝑉
or 𝑑𝑉∕𝑑𝑄) from discrete sampling points can act as a high-pass 
filter, which may amplify sensor noise. Consequently, these meth-
ods require heavy smoothing preprocessing, introducing latency 
and computational overhead.
• Raw Data and Geometric Features: While utilizing raw charging 
curves directly avoids feature engineering, it may often lead to 
input redundancy. Geometric features, such as the time inter-
vals of equal discharge voltage difference (TIEDVD) (Liu et al., 
2013), voltage integral areas (Zhang et al., 2024a), and novel 
health indicators Zhou et al. (2016) offer enhanced stability. 
Multi-feature fusion strategies have also been widely explored Lin 
et al. (2022), Son et al. (2022), with recent works investigating 
automated feature selection Li and Chen (2025). However, prior 
works typically employ empirically fixed voltage windows, often 
lacking a systematic mechanism to adapt to the specific aging 
fingerprints of different battery chemistries, such as Nickel Cobalt 
Manganese (NCM) versus Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP).
2.2.2. Deep learning architectures
Deep learning has transformed SOH estimation by enabling end-to-
end feature learning.

• RNNs and CNNs: Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particu-
larly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit 
(GRU) networks, represent the standard for time-series forecast-
ing (Ma et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2023). Despite their success, the 
sequential nature of RNNs limits parallelization during training 
and inference, limiting throughput. Convolutional Neural Net-
works (CNNs) (Lee et al., 2023) facilitate parallel processing but 
are constrained by local receptive fields, making it difficult to 
capture global degradation trends over long operational cycles. 
Hybrid CNN-based frameworks have been combined with random 
forests Yang et al. (2022), while ensemble learning paradigms 
further enhance estimation robustness Zhang et al. (2024b). Ad-
ditionally, LSTM-based models leveraging incremental capacity 
features Zhang et al. (2023) remain a strong baseline for battery 
health prognostics.
• Transformers and Attention Mechanisms: The introduction of the 
Transformer architecture (Hannan et al., 2021) addressed the 
limitations of RNNs and CNNs by utilizing self-attention to model 
long-range dependencies. Hybrid models, such as the
CNN-Transformer (Bai and Wang, 2023; Gu et al., 2023) and 
BiGRU-Transformer Jia et al. (2023), have further improved accu-
racy. Vision Transformer Chen et al. (2023) and temporal fusion 
Transformer Gomez et al. (2024) architectures have also been 
explored for battery health estimation. More recently, efficient 
Transformer variants such as BMSformer Li et al. (2024) have 
been specifically designed for online SOH estimation. However, 
the standard Dot-Product Attention mechanism involves a time 
and memory complexity of 𝑂(𝑁2) with respect to sequence length 
𝑁. This quadratic cost presents a bottleneck for deployment 
on edge devices (Xiong et al., 2019). Moreover, as noted in 
recent theoretical studies (Chen, 2023), deep attention layers 
may be prone to rank collapse, a phenomenon where the output 
feature matrix loses expressiveness, limiting the model’s ability to 
distinguish fine-grained aging patterns.
To address these limitations, this study introduces SL-AgentNet. 
By replacing quadratic attention with a linear-complexity 𝑂(𝑁) Agent 
Attention mechanism and integrating systematic feature selection, our 
framework aims to address the trilemma of noise sensitivity, computa-
tional burden, and feature expressiveness.
3. Methodology framework and data foundation
3.1. Overall estimation framework
SOH is a key indicator of battery degradation, quantifying the 
ability of a battery to store and deliver energy relative to its fresh state. 
While universal standardization remains absent, capacity retention per-
sists as the most widely adopted operational proxy (Lu et al., 2023). In 
this study, SOH is defined as the ratio of the current maximum available 
capacity (𝐶current) to the rated nominal capacity (𝐶rated): 
SOH = 𝐶current
𝐶rated
× 100%
(1)
Although SOH can also be characterized by the increase in internal 
resistance, this study primarily focuses on the capacity fade dimension 
as the primary metric for energy storage capability.
Given that direct capacity measurement is often impractical for 
online BMS due to the necessity of full charge–discharge cycles, a 
data-driven framework, depicted in Fig. 1, is proposed. This systematic 
methodology comprises four strategic phases:
1. Data Acquisition: This phase entails extracting raw voltage, cur-
rent, and time series data from charging/discharging cycles 
across diverse battery chemistries.
2. Systematic Feature Engineering: A multi-scale sliding window
strategy is applied to identify robust Health Indicators (HIs) from 
partial voltage segments, as detailed in Section 3.3.
3. Model Development: The proposed SL-AgentNet is trained using 
a historical reference hyperparameter calibration strategy to 
promote efficiency and accuracy.
4. Performance Evaluation: The final phase validates the model’s 
estimation precision, computational efficiency, and robustness 
against baselines on unseen test cells.
3.2. Experimental datasets
To evaluate the generalization capability of the proposed method 
across varying chemistries and operational profiles, three authoritative 
public datasets were utilized. The specifications are summarized as 
follows:
• Oxford Dataset (Pouch, Lithium Cobalt Oxide (LCO)-based): Pro-
vided by the Oxford Battery Intelligence Lab (Birkl et al., 2017), 
this dataset includes 8 pouch cells (0.74 Ah) tested at 40 ◦C. It is 
worth noting that it employs the Urban Artemis drive cycle for the 
discharging phase. Although our Health Indicators are primarily 
derived from the stable constant-current charging phase, this 
dynamic discharging profile induces realistic degradation patterns 
distinct from standard constant-current cycling, thereby making 
it a rigorous benchmark for evaluating model robustness under 
dynamic load history.
• NASA Dataset (18650, Nickel Cobalt Aluminum (NCA)): Sourced 
from the NASA Prognostics Center of Excellence (Xu et al.), this 
dataset includes 4 cylindrical cells (B0005–B0018) with a rated 
capacity of 2.0 Ah, cycled at 24 ◦C. A distinct characteristic of 
this dataset is the significant capacity regeneration phenomena, 
manifested as local fluctuations, and varying discharge currents. 
These features provide a rigorous test for the model’s stability and 
temporal feature extraction capabilities.
• CALCE Dataset (Prismatic, LCO): Provided by the Center for Ad-
vanced Life Cycle Engineering (CALCE) at the University of Mary-
land Cadini et al. (2019), this dataset features 4 prismatic cells 
(CS2_35 to CS2_38) with a rated capacity of 1.1 Ah. These bat-
teries exhibit a rapid non-linear degradation tail, or ‘‘diving’’ 
behavior, in late life stages, testing the model’s ability to track 
accelerated aging trends.
The diversity in cell formats (Pouch/Cylindrical/Prismatic), rated ca-
pacities (0.74–2.0 Ah), and cathode chemistries (LCO-based/NCA) fa-
cilitates a comprehensive validation of the proposed framework.

3.3.2. Multi-scale sliding window screening strategy
Although voltage integration is established in the literature, prior 
studies typically rely on static, empirically defined windows. Battery 
degradation mechanisms, however, are heterogeneous; consequently, 
the voltage region containing the most salient aging information varies 
significantly across chemistries.
To address this limitation, the Systematic Screening Framework is 
introduced (Fig. 3). To adhere to prognostic standards and minimize 
the risk of data leakage, this process is designed as an offline calibra-
tion step, performed exclusively on available historical reference data 
(i.e., the training partition) prior to model deployment. The procedure 
involves three rigorous steps:

the subsequent testing and inference phases. The calibration 
analysis revealed distinct optimal intervals for different datasets:
• For the Oxford dataset, the full-range CCCA was identi-
fied as the optimal fixed input. This suggests a uniform 
capacity fade mechanism where the global integral yields 
a favorable signal-to-noise ratio.
• For the CALCE dataset, the calibration pointed to the par-
tial segment (3.4–3.8 V CCDA), indicating that aging is 
locally concentrated in specific phase transitions for this 
chemistry.
• For the NASA dataset, the left section of the CCDA curve 
was selected. This segmentation was based on the first volt-
age minimum point (as depicted in Fig. 2(i)), identifying 
the early discharge phase as the effective indicator.
This process allows the feature extraction rules to be defined
a priori, simulating a realistic BMS deployment where the op-
timal observation window is pre-configured based on battery 
characteristics.
3.3.3. Novelty and applicability
The core innovation of this section is the transition from static 
feature selection to a data-driven hyperparameter search. By treating 
the feature interval as a learnable hyperparameter derived during the 
offline calibration phase, our framework facilitates the delivery of 
highly informative input to the subsequent SL-AgentNet.

4.3. The proposed AFF module
To circumvent the limitations of single-view feature extraction and 
capture diverse degradation patterns, the Attention Feature Fusion 
(AFF) module is proposed. Functioning as the core feature extractor,

5. Results and discussion
This section presents a systematic evaluation of the proposed SL-
AgentNet framework, benchmarked against four baseline models, in-

test horizon for prognostic evaluation.
In addition to reporting estimation accuracy, this section elucidates 
the mechanistic origins of performance gains, verifies model robustness 
through rigorous ablation and sensitivity analyses, quantifies prediction 
uncertainty, and addresses the limitations of the current approach. This 
holistic assessment validates the practical viability of the framework for 
resource-constrained vehicular instrumentation.

5.3. Comparison on Oxford dataset: Handling abrupt degradation
Fig. 11 and Table 7 present the estimation results on the Oxford 
dataset. While all models capture the general linear decay trend, a gran-
ular analysis of the degradation trajectories reveals critical differences 
in dynamic response characteristics.
5.3.1. Responsiveness to instantaneous drops (Cells 2 & 6)
A challenge inherent to the Oxford dataset is the presence of abrupt 
SOH drops or local knee-points, which are particularly visible in Cell 2 
and Cell 6.
• Baseline Limitations (Hysteresis Effect): As evidenced by the mag-
nified regions of Fig. 11, sequential models such as the LSTM 
and CNN-LSTM often suffer from a pronounced hysteresis effect, 
or phase lag. Constrained by recurrent memory inertia, these 
architectures may struggle to synchronize with rapid downward 
transitions. This results in a negative time lag and a consistent 
underestimation of the instantaneous degradation magnitude.
• SL-AgentNet Advantage: In contrast, SL-AgentNet exhibits
improved temporal alignment, closely tracking these abrupt drops 
with minimal lag. Mechanistically, this responsiveness is driven 
by the S-DSConv module embedded within the FLFA block. By ex-
ecuting convolution operations prior to attention aggregation, the 
S-DSConv acts as a high-frequency feature extractor. This com-
ponent remains highly sensitive to immediate gradient changes, 
allowing the model to adapt rapidly to local structural shifts 
without the memory inertia typical of RNNs.
5.3.2. Precision in linear decay (Cells 1, 3, 7, & 8)
For cells exhibiting relatively smooth linear decay, such as Cells 1, 3, 
7, and 8, the primary challenge involves minimizing steady-state error 
and jitter caused by measurement noise. SL-AgentNet achieves strong 
alignment with the ground truth, yielding 𝑅2 values exceeding 0.998; 
for instance, Cell 3 achieves 0.9990. Here, the post-fusion L-DSConv 
module plays an important role. Its large receptive field smooths out 
sensor noise while preserving the dominant aging trend. Consequently, 
SL-AgentNet achieves a lower average MAE of 0.0029 and an RMSE 
of 0.0041, representing a 60.8% reduction in MAE compared to the 
Transformer baseline (0.0074). This demonstrates its capability to bal-
ance dynamic sensitivity via the S-DSConv with static stability via the 
L-DSConv.

• Number of Parameters (Params): Indicates the model’s archi-
tectural complexity. Fewer parameters reduce memory access 
frequency and the risk of overfitting on small datasets.
• Model Size (Storage): The actual Flash memory footprint re-
quired to store model weights, which is a stringent constraint for 
automotive microcontrollers (typically < 1 MB).
• Inference Latency: The real-world execution time required to 
process a single input sequence (micro-scale response). This de-
termines whether the model can operate within the real-time 
scheduling slot (e.g., 10 ms task cycle) of the BMS.
5.6.1. Experimental setup for latency measurement
Unlike theoretical FLOPs, inference latency is typically considered a 
primary determinant of real-time feasibility on embedded controllers. 
A standardized benchmark environment was established using a CPU 
to simulate the sequential processing characteristic of BMS MCUs, 
adhering to the following protocols:
• Device & Process: Measurements were executed on a CPU with 
a batch size of 1 to mimic the stream-processing nature of BMS 
operations. The protocol comprised 10 warm-up runs followed by 
1000 active measurement cycles to promote statistical robustness; 
only forward inference time was recorded, excluding data I/O 
overhead.
• Input Constraints: The input dimensions were fixed at (1, 𝑁, 𝐶). 
While Transformer and LSTM baselines utilized a context window 
of 𝑁= 5, SL-AgentNet employed a restricted window of 𝑁=
4 due to the architectural configuration of the Agent mecha-
nism. Achieving improved accuracy with this reduced receptive 
field—𝑁= 4 versus 𝑁= 5—highlights the enhanced efficiency of 
SL-AgentNet’s feature extraction capabilities.
5.6.2. Inference latency and real-time capability
Table 13 indicates that SL-AgentNet achieves a sub-millisecond in-
ference latency of 0.7656 ms alongside a throughput of 1306 samples/s. 
Furthermore, the model demonstrates the lowest latency standard de-
viation of 0.0907 ms among all tested architectures. This minimal vari-
ance suggests enhanced temporal stability—an important factor for sup-
porting deterministic behavior in safety-critical Real-Time Operating 
Systems (RTOS).
Although the LSTM baseline exhibits marginally lower latency (≈
0.69 ms) due to minimal recurrent overhead in short sequences, SL-
AgentNet performs comparably to the efficient CNN-LSTM (≈0.74 ms) 
and outperforms Transformer-based models (≈1.0–1.1 ms). These re-
sults indicate that the linear-complexity FLFA module mitigates the 
computational burden typically inherent in attention mechanisms.
5.6.3. Distinguishing cumulative FLOPs from low latency
A potential ambiguity arises when contrasting the high FLOPs count 
(589 M) with the minimal latency (0.77 ms). It is important to clar-
ify the physical definition of these metrics within our experimental 
framework:
• FLOPs (Macro-scale Cost): The FLOPs values reported in Table 
13 quantify the cumulative computational cost for processing a 
complete battery charge/discharge cycle. Derived from a rep-
resentative Oxford dataset cycle comprising thousands of time 
steps, this metric evaluates the total energy consumption for a 
full diagnostic event.
• Latency (Micro-scale Response): Latency measures the infer-
ence time for a single sliding window input (e.g., 𝑁
=
4), 
representing the instantaneous response time required by the BMS 
controller per execution step.
Consequently, high cumulative FLOPs do not contradict low instanta-
neous latency. Given that SOH estimation is a low-frequency prognostic 
task—typically executed once per cycle or at minute-level intervals—
the cumulative load is distributed over extended durations. This renders 
the 0.77 ms per-step latency likely compatible with automotive MCU 
background task scheduling.
5.6.4. The efficiency-accuracy trade-off
SL-AgentNet distinguishes itself through a strategic balance between 
resource consumption and estimation reliability. While the CNN-LSTM 
baseline offers the lowest computational cost (115 M FLOPs) and 
marginally faster inference (0.74 ms), this lightweight nature compro-
mises representation capacity, resulting in a relatively high RMSE of 
0.0100. In safety-critical BMS applications, an estimation error of 1% 
is frequently deemed the threshold of unreliability, potentially neces-
sitating conservative battery utilization or obscuring rapid degradation 
signals.
Conversely, SL-AgentNet exchanges a manageable increase in com-
putational load—remaining well within the sub-millisecond safety mar-
gin of 0.77 ms—for enhanced performance. By reducing the RMSE to 
0.0042, our model achieves a 58% error reduction relative to CNN-
LSTM. This trade-off is justifiable for modern electric vehicles: the 
incremental increase in storage (40 KB vs. 15 KB) and FLOPs is neg-
ligible for automotive-grade MCUs, whereas the improved accuracy 
contributes to extended battery lifespan and safer operational limits. 
Thus, SL-AgentNet provides an effective solution where precision is 
paramount, yet hardware constraints remain respected.
5.6.5. Implementation challenges and hardware validation
Deploying deep learning models on embedded systems requires 
addressing specific hardware limitations, particularly regarding non-
linear operations.
Floating-point unit (FPU) limitations. The proposed PIAF module uti-
lizes heterogeneous activation functions (e.g., Tanh, GeLU) involving 
exponential calculations (𝑒𝑥). On low-power automotive MCUs, these 
operations can be computationally expensive and may induce latency 
jitter.
Hardware-in-the-loop validation. To validate the feasibility of the pro-
posed framework, we conducted deployment experiments on a TI 
Cortex-M4 microcontroller at Sichuan SEVCLAB Testing Technol-
ogy Co., Ltd. (a subsidiary of the Tsinghua University Prof. Ouyang 
Minggao Academician Workstation).
• Look-Up Table (LUT) Optimization: We implemented the
exponential-heavy activations using LUTs with piecewise linear 
interpolation. This is a standard industry practice to balance 
precision and speed.
• Experimental Results: Comparative testing between the PC-
based floating-point inference and the MCU-based LUT imple-
mentation confirms that the optimization significantly reduces 
cycle counts. Crucially, the SOH estimation discrepancy (accuracy 
loss) introduced by this approximation is controlled strictly within
0.3%, which is negligible for BMS applications.

6.1. Statistical significance and effect size
Paired t-tests were performed to verify performance differences, 
with the Bonferroni correction applied to maintain robustness against 
multiple comparisons (𝛼′ ≈0.003). The results are detailed in Table 16.
NASA dataset (Strong generalization). SL-AgentNet shows strong perfor-
mance on the NASA dataset. Compared to both LSTM and Transformer 
baselines, our model achieves significant reductions in MAE (𝑝< 0.001) 
with large effect sizes (Cohen’s 𝑑> 0.8). Regarding RMSE, while SL-
AgentNet achieves a lower mean error with a large effect size (𝑑= 2.07
vs. Transformer), the 𝑝-value (0.026) did not reach the Bonferroni 
threshold. This suggests that while the improvement magnitude is 
practically significant, statistical power is limited by the small sample 
size (𝑁= 4 cells).
CALCE dataset (Engineering equivalence). On the CALCE dataset, SL-
AgentNet maintains a significant lead over the Transformer in MAE 
(𝑝< 0.001). Compared to the LSTM baseline, the analysis reveals:
1. RMSE Equivalence: No statistically significant difference was 
observed (𝑝= 0.332), indicating comparable performance in 
minimizing large squared errors.
2. MAE Engineering Equivalence: Although LSTM shows a slight 
statistical preference in MAE (𝑝< 0.001), the mean difference is 
merely −0.0014 (0.14% SOH), which is negligible compared to 
typical sensor noise.
Thus, SL-AgentNet demonstrates engineering equivalence to LSTM

7. Conclusion
This study presents a practical, computationally efficient framework 
for battery SOH estimation, designed to address the stringent opera-
tional constraints of vehicular BMS. By recognizing the limitations of 
direct measurement and the high computational cost of standard deep 
learning models, a dual-pronged solution comprising systematic feature 
selection and a lightweight network architecture was developed.
The proposed SL-AgentNet is characterized by its linear compu-
tational complexity. By integrating the AFF module with the Fast 
Local–Global Fusion Attention (FLFA) mechanism, the model captures 
long-range degradation dependencies while addressing the quadratic 
computational overhead (𝑂(𝑁2)) associated with traditional Trans-
formers. Furthermore, the incorporation of FDFA and PIAF modules 
enhances feature diversity and non-linear representation, facilitating 
high estimation accuracy within a lightweight architecture.
Experimental results on the Oxford, NASA, and CALCE datasets 
indicate that SL-AgentNet demonstrates enhanced performance com-
pared to baseline models, including the LSTM and CNN-Transformer. 
Specifically, the model achieves significant accuracy gains, with MAE 
reductions reaching 74.78% on the Oxford dataset compared to the 
CNN-Transformer baseline. Crucially, this competitive performance is 
realized with a parameter count of only 6114 and a storage footprint of 
40.92 KB. Hardware-in-the-loop validation on a TI Cortex-M4 MCU has 
further confirmed the model’s engineering feasibility, where LUT opti-
mization ensured real-time responsiveness with a negligible accuracy 
loss of within 0.3%.
Despite these advancements, limitations remain regarding the re-
liance on stable constant-current segments for feature extraction. Fu-
ture work aims to extend the framework to encompass a wider range
